7. Vascular structure and wall mechanics

The same vessel simultaneously carries a flow, contains a pressure and sustains a solid-wall stress. Its radius couples all three. A larger lumen can lower hydraulic resistance while increasing the wall tension needed to contain a specified pressure. Understanding the vascular network therefore requires more than replacing each vessel by an isolated rigid pipe.

Lessons 13 and 14 complete the course with vascular anatomy, function, regional structure and a constitutive-modeling workflow. Both full local machine transcripts were read. The source leaves inflation/extension boundary-value problems for further study; this chapter supplies original complete examples, with explicit geometry and limits. No individual artery, histology image or clinical pressure reading is calibrated by these constructions.

A branch network must conserve flow at the right level L13 · 03:41 L14 · 01:15

Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins return it. These names concern direction of transport, not an absolute oxygen label: pulmonary vessels provide the familiar reversal of the usual systemic oxygen association. Arterioles connect the distributing arteries to exchange networks, and capillaries drain through venules into veins. The NCI vessel-structure resource describes this hierarchy and its differences in wall construction.

At a junction with negligible storage or leakage, $Q_{\rm parent}=\sum_jQ_j$. For an incompressible rigid individual vessel, $Q=A\bar u$, where $\bar u$ is cross-sectional mean velocity, not the centreline velocity. Across an entire parallel bed, use its total perfused cross-sectional area. One cannot equate the flow in the aorta to that of one capillary and then compare their individual areas.

For an original scale calculation, take total flow $5\;\mathrm{L/min}$ and $10^9$ identical parallel channels of radius $4\;\mu\mathrm m$. The total channel area is $N\pi r^2=0.050265482\;\mathrm{m^2}$. Dividing $Q=8.333333\times10^{-5}\;\mathrm{m^3/s}$ by that area gives $\bar u=0.001657864\;\mathrm{m/s}$ and a 1-mm transit time of $0.603186$ s. These hypothetical counts and dimensions illustrate the algebra, not a measurement of every human capillary or its flow distribution.

A longer residence time can help exchange, but slow flow does not by itself prove complete equilibration. For transverse diffusion distance $\delta$ and diffusivity $D_m$, compare $\delta^2/D_m$ with transit time $L/\bar u$. Their ratio $D_mL/(\bar u\delta^2)$ measures the available diffusion time on this simplified basis. Permeability, surface area, chemical binding and tissue demand also matter. The source's exchange-site picture is a functional motivation, not a complete oxygen transport model.

Parallel pathways also matter for resistance. For $N$ equal rigid Newtonian tubes with resistance $R_v$, the bed resistance is $R_v/N$. At fixed pressure drop, increasing every radius by 20% multiplies each conductance and total flow by $1.2^4=2.0736$ under the Poiseuille assumptions. If only one of two initially equal beds dilates and total supply is instead fixed, the dilated bed obtains the fraction $2.0736/(1+2.0736)=0.674648621$ of that supply. The supply constraint changes the inference.

Pressure, its gradient and its oscillation are different observables L13 · 08:12 L14 · 08:58

Pressure at a location is a scalar field $p(\mathbf x,t)$; a pressure gradient is force per volume, $-\nabla p$. A time trace ranging between two pressures does not supply a spatial gradient without a distance and a second location. Likewise, the number $80$ in an illustrative 120/80 pressure pair is not automatically the axial pressure drop driving flow along a given segment. A uniform offset in both ends' pressure changes wall loading without changing their difference.

The wall responds strongly to transmural pressure $\Delta p=p_i-p_o$, with internal and external pressures specified at the same axial level and time. The flow responds to spatial differences of pressure and other body/inertial forces. Consider a vessel with $p_i=15$ kPa and $p_o=2$ kPa. Its transmural load is 13 kPa. Raising both by 5 kPa leaves this difference unchanged. A flow model that uses only an upstream absolute pressure still needs downstream and surrounding conditions.

The lecture illustrates attenuation of pulsations through the network. This is a useful trend, not a guarantee of exactly steady flow in every arteriole, capillary or vein. Compliance, distributed resistance, inertance and wave reflections all affect the waveform. Respiratory variation, vessel motion and local regulation add further timescales. It is safer to say that a mean-flow approximation may be adequate for a specified observable than to declare an entire vessel class mathematically steady.

Even a simple reservoir has only finite attenuation. If compliance $C_V=dV/dp$ feeds a resistance $R$ toward a constant reference pressure, then $C_V\dot p=Q_{\rm in}-(p-p_v)/R$. A sinusoidal input at angular frequency $\omega$ gives an outflow-amplitude ratio $[1+(\omega R C_V)^2]^{-1/2}$ relative to the input. It tends towards zero at high frequency but is not exactly zero for finite positive parameters. The time constant $RC_V$ has units seconds. Chapter 3 derived the associated decay and energy storage.

The wall is a regional, layered and active material L13 · 13:42 L14 · 17:28

In a representative artery, the lumen is bounded by intima, media and adventitia. Endothelium faces the blood. Elastin, collagen, smooth muscle and other constituents differ among layers and along the network. Those differences motivate region-specific constitutive parameters and sometimes different model forms. They also make a single scalar modulus an incomplete description of large-deformation behavior.

Elastic arteries near the heart provide substantial compliance. Muscular arteries and arterioles actively influence distribution through smooth-muscle tone, with neural, circulating and local signals. The regulation is not exclusively a command sent from the brain. Veins accommodate substantial volume at relatively low pressure and can have valves that limit reverse flow. Their storage behavior depends on compliance, geometry and tone; it is not caused by valves alone. External muscle action and respiratory pressure differences can contribute to venous return.

Capillaries have thin exchange barriers, rather than the fully developed three-layer structure of a large artery. The smallest arterioles do retain functionally important smooth muscle, so the source's informal “one and a half layers” should not become a universal histological law that removes the very cells responsible for resistance control. The approximate diameter bands in the lecture are teaching classifications, not sharp universal boundaries.

Likewise, a histological cross-section showing a collapsed vein and a round artery is not an in-vivo constitutive experiment. Fixation, unloading and surrounding support affect shape. The solid tissue can be modeled as a continuum even when the fluid description needs cell-scale or mixture refinement; the geometric cell-size warning from chapter 6 is not a prohibition on all small-vessel mechanics.

Wall shear and hoop stress live in different materials

At a fluid–wall interface, the fluid traction has both a normal pressure contribution and a tangential viscous contribution. The wall itself must carry internal solid stresses that balance these tractions together with external loads. A fluid wall-shear map therefore does not directly report circumferential wall tension or a tissue failure margin.

For a thin cylindrical membrane of current inner radius $r$, thickness $h$ and length $L$, cut a longitudinal half-cylinder. The internal pressure resultant normal to the cut is $2rL\Delta p$. Two cut-wall sections supply approximately $2hL\sigma_\theta$. Thus

\[\sigma_\theta\simeq\frac{\Delta p\,r}{h}.\]

Here $\sigma_\theta$ is circumferential normal stress in the solid, in Pa. The tension resultant per axial length is $N_\theta=h\sigma_\theta\simeq\Delta p\,r$, in N/m. The familiar closed-end axial formula $\sigma_z\simeq\Delta p\,r/(2h)$ additionally assumes how end pressure is supported; tethering, imposed length and external axial forces alter that condition.

For steady fully developed Newtonian fluid flow in the same rigid circular lumen, the fluid shear magnitude is $\tau_w=4\mu Q/(\pi r^3)$. In the original example $\Delta p=13300$ Pa, $r=3$ mm and $h=0.3$ mm, the membrane estimate is $133000$ Pa. With $\mu=0.0035\;\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ and $Q=1\;\mathrm{mL/s}$, the fluid shear is only $0.165050$ Pa. Their different orders of magnitude do not imply that the small shear is biologically unimportant: endothelial signaling and structural containment are different mechanisms.

Doubling radius at fixed pressure and thickness doubles the membrane stress. Doubling radius at fixed Newtonian flow reduces the shear by eight. At fixed pressure gradient and viscosity, however, Poiseuille flow grows as $r^4$ and wall shear grows as $r$. Every scaling statement needs its held-fixed variables. Aneurysm rupture cannot be inferred from one of these algebraic scalings without material strength, wall structure, geometry and loading data.

A thick wall requires a stress distribution, not one representative value

In an axisymmetric circular tube with no shear and negligible body forces, radial equilibrium is

\[\frac{d\sigma_r}{dr}+\frac{\sigma_r-\sigma_\theta}{r}=0.\]

Boundary tractions are $\sigma_r(a)=-p_i$ and $\sigma_r(b)=-p_o$. Rearrangement gives $d(r\sigma_r)/dr=\sigma_\theta$, so the exact thickness integral is

\[\int_a^b\sigma_\theta\,dr=p_i a-p_o b.\]

At zero external gauge pressure, the thickness-averaged hoop stress is therefore $p_i a/(b-a)$. This force-balance result does not make the local hoop stress constant. The thin-membrane expression can approximate a representative value while missing a higher inner-wall stress.

As an original small-deformation, homogeneous isotropic linear-elastic control, the Lamé stresses have the form $\sigma_r=A_L-B_L/r^2$ and $\sigma_\theta=A_L+B_L/r^2$. At $p_o=0$,

\[A_L=\frac{p_i a^2}{b^2-a^2},\qquad B_L=\frac{p_i a^2b^2}{b^2-a^2}.\]

For $a=3$ mm, $b=4.5$ mm and $p_i=13300$ Pa, the inner-wall hoop stress is $34580$ Pa while its thickness average is $26600$ Pa. Using the average as the inner-wall value would underestimate that local value by $23.0769\%$. These fields are a linear-elastic idealization, not a nonlinear layered-artery solution. Different constitutive laws, axial constraints and residual stresses alter the distribution while the radial force-balance integral remains essential.

The wall is generally not stress-free when excised and unloaded. A radial cut can release residual strain, and a ring may open. An open-sector reference is one modeling construction; it does not prove that every layer and direction becomes simultaneously stress-free after one cut. The Holzapfel–Gasser–Ogden 2000 primary abstract explicitly separates a load-free closed tube from an assumed relaxed open sector and studies its influence on wall stress.

Solve an incompressible finite-inflation boundary-value problem L13 · 25:42 L14 · 32:36

Take a closed, stress-free, isotropic neo-Hookean reference tube with radii $A,B$ and impose a constant axial stretch $\lambda_z>0$. These are deliberately stronger assumptions than an actual residually stressed artery. Seek the semi-inverse map $r=r(R)$, $\theta=\Theta$, $z=\lambda_zZ$. Its principal stretches are

\[\lambda_r=\frac{dr}{dR},\qquad \lambda_\theta=\frac rR,\qquad \lambda_z.\]

Incompressibility gives $(dr/dR)(r/R)\lambda_z=1$. Integrating between the inner radii yields

\[r^2=a^2+\frac{R^2-A^2}{\lambda_z},\qquad b^2-a^2=\frac{B^2-A^2}{\lambda_z}.\]

Thus an expanding wall becomes thinner in a geometry-dependent way; fixed material volume does not mean fixed thickness. For energy $W=\mu_s(I_1-3)/2$ per reference volume, the Cauchy stress is $\boldsymbol\sigma=-p_s\mathbf I+\mu_s\mathbf B$. The multiplier $p_s$ is a solid constraint pressure, distinct from the lumen pressure $p_i$. Subtracting radial stress from hoop stress eliminates it:

\[\sigma_\theta-\sigma_r=\mu_s(\lambda_\theta^2-\lambda_r^2).\]

Integrating radial equilibrium determines the required transmural pressure:

\[p_i-p_o=\int_a^b\frac{\mu_s(\lambda_\theta^2-\lambda_r^2)}r\,dr.\]

For prescribed geometry and axial stretch this integral is the pressure prediction. For prescribed pressure it becomes a nonlinear equation for $a$; one must examine solution existence, stability and parameter sensitivity rather than assume a unique physically stable radius.

At $\lambda_z=1$, integration gives a closed form that can be checked independently:

\[\Delta p=\mu_s\left[\ln\frac{Ba}{Ab}+\frac{a^2-A^2}{2}\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}\right)\right].\]

With $A=3$ mm, $B=3.6$ mm, $a=4.5$ mm and $\mu_s=100000$ Pa, $b=4.920366$ mm and $\Delta p=13845.183844$ Pa. The code compares numerical quadrature of the original equilibrium integral with this analytic expression and checks $J=1$. At $a=A$, $b=B$ and pressure vanishes. At arbitrarily large inflation, this simplified model approaches $\mu_s\ln(B/A)$ rather than stiffening without bound. That is a limitation of the chosen material idealization, not a prediction of a safe anatomical pressure or a universal rupture threshold.

The directional arguments create anisotropy, not the exponential alone L13 · 27:10 L14 · 35:05

A scalar energy can be nonlinear and isotropic: $\exp[k(I_1-3)^2]$ remains isotropic because its argument contains no material direction. Exponentials are useful for strong stiffening, but are not a test for anisotropy. The latter enters through reference directions, directional invariants or other structural tensors with a defined transformation rule.

For an original symmetric two-family illustration, set $\mathbf f_{0\pm}=\cos\alpha\,\mathbf e_\Theta\pm\sin\alpha\,\mathbf e_Z$, where $\alpha$ is measured from the circumferential direction. Let $I_{4\pm}=\mathbf f_{0\pm}\cdot\mathbf C\mathbf f_{0\pm}$ and choose

\[W=\frac{\mu_s}{2}(I_1-3)+\sum_{q=+,-}\frac{k_1}{2k_2}\left\{\exp\left[k_2\langle I_{4q}-1\rangle_+^2\right]-1\right\}.\]

The positive part $\langle x\rangle_+=\max(x,0)$ implements a specified tension-only response. The coefficients $\mu_s,k_1$ have units Pa, while $k_2>0$ is dimensionless. This is a teaching member of a fibre-reinforced family, not an exact fitted reconstruction of a lecture energy. Layer-specific parameters, fibre dispersion, active smooth muscle and damage would require additional choices and data.

For diagonal inflation/extension stretches, both families have $I_{4q}=\lambda_\theta^2\cos^2\alpha+\lambda_z^2\sin^2\alpha$. Their equal and opposite shear contributions cancel in an axisymmetric state, but their normal contributions remain directional. On the tensile branch with $e=I_4-1$, $W_{I_4}=k_1e\exp(k_2e^2)$. Two families contribute $4k_1e\exp(k_2e^2)\lambda_\theta\cos^2\alpha$ to $\partial W/\partial\lambda_\theta$. The original example $\alpha=30^\circ$, $\lambda_\theta=1.2$, $\lambda_z=1.1$, $k_1=10000$ Pa and $k_2=10$ gives $I_4=1.3825$ and nominal circumferential contribution $59474.926046$ Pa. A finite-difference energy derivative verifies it.

The primary 2000 model uses separate media/adventitia fibre structure and considers combined inflation, extension and torsion. Its abstract supports the architecture, not our parameter values. The source's named tissue-specific exponential forms should similarly be read as calibrated proposals within particular tests, rather than a rule assigning one universal energy to every aorta or carotid.

Pressure waves connect tangent wall compliance to flow

Let $A_0$ be a uniform reference lumen area, $u$ a small axial fluid-velocity perturbation and $a$ the small area perturbation. Neglect mean flow, viscous damping, wall inertia and distributed forcing in a long-wave tube model. Linearized incompressible continuity and axial momentum are

\[\partial_ta+A_0\partial_xu=0,\qquad \rho\partial_tu+\partial_xp=0.\]

If the local wall law has positive tangent area compliance $C_A=dA/dp$, so $a=C_Ap$ for these perturbations, eliminate $u$ to obtain $\partial_{tt}p=c^2\partial_{xx}p$ with

\[c^2=\frac{A_0}{\rho C_A}.\]

The speed of the pressure disturbance is not the mean material speed $Q/A$. A pulse can travel several metres per second while blood particles move much more slowly. It is also a tangent property at a loading state: a nonlinear wall can have a different wave speed at a different mean pressure.

For a thin, linearly elastic incremental wall with an effective circumferential modulus $E_{\rm eff}$ incorporating the stated axial constraint, $dr/r\simeq r\,dp/(E_{\rm eff}h)$. Since $dA/A=2dr/r$, the reduced expression is $c^2\simeq E_{\rm eff}h/(2\rho r)$. This is not a universal formula with every possible Poisson constraint hidden. With $E_{\rm eff}=500000$ Pa, $h=0.5$ mm, $r=5$ mm and $\rho=1060\;\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $c=4.856429\;\mathrm{m/s}$. Quadrupling modulus at fixed geometry doubles it.

The Coccarelli and colleagues 2021 primary study connects a three-dimensional fibre-reinforced wall law to reduced one-dimensional flow and examines compliance and structural sensitivity. Its abstract emphasizes reference geometry and opening angle as influential variables. That connection motivates our tangent calculation; neither its carotid simulations nor its sensitivity analysis is reproduced here.

Calibration, prediction and adaptation are separate tasks L13 · 22:43 L14 · 30:39

The source organizes modeling as delineating general characteristics, establishing a framework, identifying a model form, calculating parameters and evaluating predictive capability. The order matters. Calling a wall incompressible, passive and hyperelastic is a set of reductions. It is not evidence that perfusion, viscoelasticity, smooth-muscle activation or growth are absent from living tissue.

In a semi-inverse calculation we propose a deformation compatible with the geometry and then require it to satisfy field equations and boundary conditions. Guessing circular inflation is only the first step; the pressure integral, axial force condition, material constraint and possible residual reference must also hold. A measured pressure–radius curve alone can trade off stiffness, stress-free geometry, axial tethering and residual strain. Adding axial force, biaxial loading, torsion or independent structure measurements can challenge that ambiguity.

After calibration, prediction should use conditions held apart from the fitted data. Repeating the calibration test more finely is useful for numerical verification but does not independently validate transfer to a new loading mode. A converged residual checks the solved equations; it does not by itself show that the chosen equations describe the artery.

On longer timescales, wall composition can adapt. The Humphrey–Rajagopal 2003 constrained-mixture primary abstract distinguishes immediate vasoactive response from growth/remodeling under sustained flow change. A passive elastic snapshot cannot predict that evolution without constituent turnover, deposition states and regulation laws. This is an important bridge from mechanics to mechanobiology and an informative limit of the short course's final constitutive framework.

import numpy as np
from math import pi,sqrt,log,cos,sin,exp

Q=5e-3/60;N=10**9;r_cap=4e-6;area=N*pi*r_cap*r_cap
speed=Q/area
print(f'Parallel capillary toy: total area={area:.9f} m^2; mean speed={speed:.9f} m/s; 1mm transit={.001/speed:.6f} s')
conductance_gain=1.2**4
print(f'One dilated equal bed: fixed-pressure flow gain={conductance_gain:.6f}; fixed-total share={conductance_gain/(1+conductance_gain):.9f}')
p,r,t,mu,q=13300.,.003,.0003,.0035,1e-6
hoop=p*r/t;wall_shear=4*mu*q/(pi*r**3)
print(f'Thin wall hoop/fluid shear: {hoop:.6f}/{wall_shear:.6f} Pa')
# Linear-elastic thick cylinder at a stated small-deformation geometry.
a,b=.003,.0045;Aconst=p*a*a/(b*b-a*a);Bconst=p*a*a*b*b/(b*b-a*a)
rgrid=np.linspace(a,b,100001)
sigma_r=Aconst-Bconst/rgrid**2;sigma_theta=Aconst+Bconst/rgrid**2
assert abs(sigma_r[0]+p)<1e-10 and abs(sigma_r[-1])<1e-10
average=np.trapezoid(sigma_theta,rgrid)/(b-a)
assert abs(average-p*a/(b-a))<1e-5
print(f'Thick cylinder: inner hoop={sigma_theta[0]:.6f} Pa; thickness average={average:.6f} Pa')
# Incompressible neo-Hookean tube, fixed axial stretch1, stress-free closed reference.
Ar,Br,ai,modulus=.003,.0036,.0045,100000.
bo=sqrt(Br*Br+ai*ai-Ar*Ar)
rgrid=np.linspace(ai,bo,100001);Rref=np.sqrt(rgrid*rgrid+Ar*Ar-ai*ai)
lamtheta=rgrid/Rref;lamr=Rref/rgrid
assert np.allclose(lamtheta*lamr,1.)
pquad=np.trapezoid(modulus*(lamtheta**2-lamr**2)/rgrid,rgrid)
panalytic=modulus*(log(Br*ai/(Ar*bo))+(ai*ai-Ar*Ar)/2*(1/ai**2-1/bo**2))
assert abs(pquad-panalytic)<1e-5
assert abs(modulus*(log(Br*Ar/(Ar*Br))))<1e-12
print(f'Neo-Hookean inflation: current outer radius={bo*1000:.6f} mm; pressure={panalytic:.6f} Pa')
# Original symmetric two-fibre energy: orientation, not exponential alone, carries anisotropy.
alpha=pi/6;lt,lz=1.2,1.1;k1,k2=10000.,10.
fibre_I=lambda x,z:x*x*cos(alpha)**2+z*z*sin(alpha)**2
Wf=lambda x,z:k1/k2*(exp(k2*max(fibre_I(x,z)-1,0.)**2)-1)
e=fibre_I(lt,lz)-1
nominal=4*k1*e*exp(k2*e*e)*lt*cos(alpha)**2
h=1e-6;finite=(Wf(lt+h,lz)-Wf(lt-h,lz))/(2*h)
assert abs(finite-nominal)<1e-3
print(f'Two fibres: I4={fibre_I(lt,lz):.6f}; nominal circumferential contribution={nominal:.6f} Pa')
E,hwall,radius,rho=500000.,.0005,.005,1060.
c=sqrt(E*hwall/(2*rho*radius))
print(f'Linear tube wave speed={c:.6f} m/s; fourfold modulus speed={2*c:.6f} m/s')
print('All conservation, stress, inflation, fibre derivative and wave checks passed.')

Problems and fully worked solutions

1. Sum the right area. Ten identical parallel channels carry total flow $Q$. If each has area $A_c$, what are the equal channel flows and velocities? What error follows from assigning total $Q$ to every channel?

Solution

Each carries $Q/10$, so its mean speed is $Q/(10A_c)$. This equals total flow divided by total area $10A_c$. Assigning $Q$ to each would predict ten times the actual speed and ten times the total supplied flow. Equal distribution is itself an assumption; unequal resistances give unequal branch flows.

2. Change the supply condition. One of two equal parallel Poiseuille beds expands its radius by20%. Find its fraction of total flow. Does its absolute flow always double?

Solution

The conductance ratio becomes $g=1.2^4=2.0736$, so its share is $g/(1+g)=0.674648621$. At fixed pressure drop its absolute flow multiplies by $g$. At fixed total flow, it grows from half the total to that fraction, a factor $2g/(1+g)=1.349297242$. A statement about conductance does not determine which external constraint is maintained.

3. Separate transmural and axial pressure. A segment has inlet/outlet lumen pressures15/14kPa and external pressure2kPa. Both lumen pressures rise by3kPa while external pressure is unchanged. What happens to the driving drop and wall loading?

Solution

The axial drop remains1kPa, but the inlet/outlet transmural loads rise from13/12kPa to16/15kPa. If geometry and material response were held fixed, the simple pressure-driven flow would remain unchanged while wall stresses increase. In an actual compliant segment, the changed geometry can feed back on flow, so this fixed-geometry conclusion is only a control calculation.

4. Compare stress types. At fixed flow, viscosity, pressure and thickness, double the radius in the thin-wall/Poiseuille estimates. Give the factors for hoop stress, fluid wall shear and hydraulic resistance.

Solution

Hoop stress scales as $r$ and doubles. Fluid wall shear scales as $r^{-3}$ and becomes one eighth. Resistance at fixed length/viscosity scales as $r^{-4}$ and becomes one sixteenth. These factors refer to different mechanical quantities and rely on the imposed pressure and flow being treated as independent held-fixed controls for their respective estimates; a coupled network would determine them together.

5. Verify a thick-wall average. Integrate radial equilibrium to derive the mean hoop stress for nonzero external pressure. Why does adding the same constant pressure to both sides change the absolute hoop stress even when transmural pressure is unchanged?

Solution

$d(r\sigma_r)/dr=\sigma_\theta$ gives $\langle\sigma_\theta\rangle=(p_i a-p_o b)/(b-a)=\Delta p\,a/(b-a)-p_o$. Raising both pressures by $c$ therefore subtracts $c$ from the mean hoop stress, an added uniform hydrostatic compression. The load differences controlling deformation of an incompressible idealization can remain unchanged while the absolute stress tensor shifts by $-c\mathbf I$. Transmural pressure alone does not define every absolute stress component.

6. Impose incompressibility. For the finite tube map, derive $b^2-a^2$ at fixed axial stretch. If $\lambda_z$ doubles while the reference wall volume is unchanged, what happens to the current wall cross-sectional area?

Solution

From $\lambda_z r\,dr=R\,dR$, integration gives $\lambda_z(b^2-a^2)=B^2-A^2$. Thus current wall area is $\pi(B^2-A^2)/\lambda_z$, which halves when axial stretch doubles. This is a kinematic constraint; pressures and stresses needed to attain that state require the constitutive law and boundary-value solution.

7. Diagnose a false anisotropy claim. Is $W=c[\exp(k(I_1-3)^2)-1]$ anisotropic merely because it is exponential? Compare it with the directional invariant in the two-family model.

Solution

The first energy depends only on the isotropic invariant $I_1$, so material rotations preserving $\mathbf C$'s eigenvalues leave its value unchanged; it is isotropic. In contrast $I_4=\mathbf f_0\cdot\mathbf C\mathbf f_0$ compares stretch with a specified material direction. Rotating the material direction relative to unequal principal stretches changes the energy. Functional nonlinearity and material symmetry are independent features.

8. Check a pulse-speed inference. In the reduced wave model, area compliance doubles at fixed density and area. What happens to wave speed? Can that speed be identified with mean blood speed?

Solution

Since $c^2=A/(\rho C_A)$, the speed becomes $c/\sqrt2$. Mean blood speed is $Q/A$ and is a different variable. The wave formula describes propagation of small coupled pressure/area disturbances under specified assumptions; no conservation identity sets it equal to particle transport speed.

9. Design a predictive wall test. A model fits pressure–radius data at one imposed axial stretch. State two measurements that could improve its identification and a separate test of predictive transfer.

Solution

Measure axial force during inflation and independently characterize unloaded geometry/residual opening or directional fibre structure; these constrain different sources of pressure–radius ambiguity. Biaxial or torsional measurements provide further independent loading modes. After fitting a declared subset, predict a held-out axial stretch or torsional loading without retuning. Numerical convergence of that prediction is necessary but separate from agreement with the held-out physical measurement. No single extra measurement guarantees unique identification of an arbitrarily rich model.
