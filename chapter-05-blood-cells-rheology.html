5. Blood cells, plasma and whole-blood rheology

A fluid can become easier to shear without storing elastic energy, and it can remember its preparation without supporting a residual shear stress after motion stops. Blood makes these distinctions unavoidable: it is a concentrated suspension of deformable, interacting cells in a protein-containing liquid. The useful question is which experiment identifies which part of that description.

This chapter develops lessons 9 and 11 into an original quantitative account. The full local machine transcripts were read, with numerical, biological and mechanical ambiguities checked separately. The alternate lesson-9 upload was also read in full and compared with lesson 9: it follows the same composition, cell-deformation and rheometry sequence, with no additional substantive topic found. Its separate playlist identity remains in the source map; machine-recognition and encoding differences do not establish byte identity. All numerical geometry, saturation, torque and transient examples below are stipulated teaching constructions, not fitted experimental data.

Define the amount of each constituent before assigning a material law L9 · 10:48 L11 · 10:56

Blood contains red cells, white cells and platelets suspended in plasma. Red cells and white cells are cells; platelets are fragments shed from megakaryocytes. The collective expression formed elements does not mean that every member is a noncellular object. Mature mammalian red cells lack nuclei and mitochondria, but retain membrane transport, enzymes and a metabolism. The lecture's structural economy argument should therefore be read as specialization for transport, not as biological inertness.

The hematocrit $H$ is the red-cell volume divided by the volume of the blood sample. It is dimensionless, although reported as a percentage. A concentration in cells per microlitre, a percentage of the count of formed elements and a volume fraction answer different questions. For a uniform illustrative suspension, if $N$ red cells have mean volume $V_c$ in sample volume $V$,

\[H=\frac{NV_c}{V}.\]

Suppose $N/V=5\times10^6\;\mathrm{cells}/\mu\mathrm L$ and $V_c=90\;\mathrm{fL/cell}$. Since $1\;\mu\mathrm L=10^9\;\mathrm{fL}$, $H=0.45$. Counting cells without their volumes would not yield this result. If other formed elements are neglected, the plasma fraction is $1-H=0.55$; that approximation does not erase their immune or clotting functions. The NHLBI CBC description distinguishes red-cell count, mean cell volume and hematocrit in precisely this way.

For a density estimate with additive constituent volumes, $\rho=H\rho_c+(1-H)\rho_p$. The density is of order $10^3\;\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, rather than the $1050\;\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ appearing in the machine transcript. The latter would exceed ordinary blood density by a factor of a thousand. A quantity such as $1.05\;\mathrm{g/cm^3}=1050\;\mathrm{kg/m^3}$ is dimensionally plausible; it is not adopted here as a universal sample measurement. Temperature, hematocrit, preparation and donor remain relevant.

Likewise, the source's rough 46% formed-element and 98% red-cell share would imply $0.46(0.98)=0.4508$ red-cell volume fraction if both are volume percentages. It would not imply 98% hematocrit. We retain the algebraic lesson without treating all reported component percentages as a verified physiological reference table.

Transport specialization still requires cellular energy L9 · 04:52 L11 · 05:45

Hemoglobin binds oxygen reversibly. A hemoglobin tetramer can bind four oxygen molecules, one per heme; after one site binds, three sites remain, not another four. Cooperative affinity is not equivalent to irreversible loading. Oxygen partial pressure, acidity, temperature and other chemical conditions influence the binding state. Carbon dioxide transport also involves bicarbonate and red-cell carbonic anhydrase; oxygen and carbon dioxide should not be pictured as simply competing for the same heme socket.

The absence of red-cell mitochondria removes mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. It does not remove glucose use. The REDS donor study on red-cell glycolysis explicitly places glycolytic ATP production at the centre of mature-cell energy maintenance. Platelets provide a sharper counterexample to the claim that all formed elements carry nutrients without consuming them: Aibibula, Naseem and Sturmey's human platelet experiments measured glucose use and oxygen consumption and found flexible glycolytic and oxidative fuel use. A transport medium is biologically active even when a reduced mechanical model suppresses that activity.

To make cooperative loading quantitative without pretending to reproduce a particular donor, stipulate a Hill saturation curve

\[S(P)=\frac{P^n}{P_{50}^n+P^n},\qquad P\ge0.\]

Require $P_{50}>0$ and $n>0$. Here $P$ and $P_{50}$ have the same pressure units, $S$ is a fraction and $n$ is a dimensionless empirical slope parameter. This formula does not derive the molecular binding sequence or force $n$ to equal four. The following logarithmic-odds identity and derivative apply for $P>0$, where $0 \[\log\frac{S}{1-S}=n\log\frac{P}{P_{50}},\qquad \frac{dS}{d\log P}=nS(1-S).\]

The sensitivity is largest at $S=1/2$, whereas the high-pressure plateau permits substantial changes of pressure with small saturation changes. Use $P_{50}=26$ in the selected pressure unit and $n=2.7$, and let the fully saturated hemoglobin-bound capacity be $C_*=20\;\mathrm{mL\ O_2/dL}$. Then $S(100)=0.974347$, $S(40)=0.761898$, and the capacity difference is $4.248975\;\mathrm{mL/dL}$. These are model outputs, not a personal oxygenation assessment. Dissolved oxygen, chemical shifts and red-cell metabolism have been omitted.

Combined with flow, the earlier oxygen balance gives $\dot M=Q(C_a-C_v)$ at steady state, with compatible volume units. A lower hematocrit can reduce hemoglobin capacity while also changing resistance. Consequently, maximizing red-cell concentration is not the same optimization problem as maximizing tissue oxygen delivery. The answer requires both transport and mechanics; a viscosity experiment alone cannot supply it.

Deformability means changing shape under constraints L9 · 06:01 L11 · 18:34

A biconcave cell has additional membrane area relative to the sphere enclosing the same volume. It is misleading to call its area the maximum possible for fixed volume: arbitrarily corrugated shapes have arbitrarily large area in an unconstrained geometric problem. The sphere minimizes area for a prescribed volume. The mechanical questions concern available area, bending, membrane shear, cytoplasmic viscosity, pressure and the timescale of passage.

For cell volume $V_c$ and membrane area $A_c$, define reduced volume

\[v_r=\frac{6\sqrt\pi V_c}{A_c^{3/2}}\le1.\]

The inequality follows from the isoperimetric bound $A_c^3\ge36\pi V_c^2$. A sphere has $v_r=1$. In an illustrative $90\;\mu\mathrm m^3$ cell with $135\;\mu\mathrm m^2$ membrane area, the equal-volume sphere needs only $97.120848\;\mu\mathrm m^2$, and $v_r=0.610194$. This available area permits shape changes that need little area dilation. It does not imply that every narrow passage is energetically easy.

An instructive geometric screen is a spherocylinder with radius $r$ and cylindrical length $\ell$, including two hemispherical ends. Its volume and area are

\[V_c=\pi r^2\ell+\frac43\pi r^3,\qquad A=2\pi r\ell+4\pi r^2=\frac{2V_c}{r}+\frac43\pi r^2.\]

At $r=1.5\;\mu\mathrm m$, the chosen volume requires $\ell=10.732395\;\mu\mathrm m$ and $A=129.424778\;\mu\mathrm m^2$, within the area budget. At $r=1.3\;\mu\mathrm m$, it requires $145.540594\;\mu\mathrm m^2$, exceeding it. This rules out that particular shape at fixed area and volume; it is not a proof that every possible configuration is impossible. The calculation also supplies no pressure threshold or membrane failure criterion.

Jay and Canham's 1977 micropipette measurements examined individual cell area and volume during aspiration into approximately $2\;\mu\mathrm m$ pipettes. Their abstract reports nearly unchanged membrane area with volume loss under the specified conditions. Thus shape accommodation in an experiment can involve water/solute transport, rather than the fixed-volume assumption of our toy screen. A model should state which constraint is imposed and which is measured.

A measured torque is an integral, not a local viscosity L9 · 13:16 L11 · 21:53

In an ideal parallel-plate rheometer, the lower plate is stationary, the upper plate rotates at angular speed $\Omega$, the gap is $h$ and the plate radius is $R$. Assume a small uniform gap, axisymmetry, no slip, negligible inertia, no edge disturbance and a sample filling the prescribed disk. The local engineering shear rate is $\dot\gamma(r)=\Omega r/h$. It vanishes at the centre and increases towards the rim. A single imposed rotation rate therefore does not produce uniform shear everywhere.

A thin annulus of area $2\pi r\,dr$ transmits tangential force $\tau(r)2\pi r\,dr$ and torque $2\pi\tau(r)r^2\,dr$. For Newtonian viscosity $\mu$,

\[T=2\pi\int_0^R\mu\frac{\Omega r}{h}r^2\,dr=\frac{\pi\mu\Omega R^4}{2h}.\]

The units are Pa times volume, or N m. In the numerical example $\mu=0.0012\;\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, $\Omega=10\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $R=0.02\;\mathrm m$ and $h=0.001\;\mathrm m$, the predicted torque is $3.015928947\times10^{-6}\;\mathrm{N\,m}$. A factor-of-ten error in gap or an imperfectly filled radius can overwhelm subtle material differences; radius enters to the fourth power.

For a steady, history-independent shear law $\tau=\tau(\dot\gamma)$, put $\dot\gamma_R=\Omega R/h$. Changing the integration variable gives

\[T=\frac{2\pi R^3}{\dot\gamma_R^3}\int_0^{\dot\gamma_R}\tau(s)s^2\,ds.\]

Differentiating with respect to $\Omega$ at fixed geometry yields the exact local rim correction

\[\tau_R=\frac{3T+\Omega\,dT/d\Omega}{2\pi R^3}.\]

This operation assumes that changing $\Omega$ samples the same single-valued constitutive curve. It can fail when structure changes with waiting time, cells migrate, wall slip occurs or a transient is treated as steady. Derivatives also magnify torque noise; smoothing choices must be reported rather than hidden.

For the original power-law example $\tau=K\dot\gamma^n$, $K$ has units Pa s$^n$, and

\[T=\frac{2\pi K}{n+3}\left(\frac\Omega h\right)^nR^{n+3},\qquad \tau_R=\frac{(n+3)T}{2\pi R^3}.\]

The Newtonian shortcut $2T/(\pi R^3)$ overestimates the true rim stress by $4/(n+3)$. At $n=1/2$ this is $1.142857$, a 14.2857% error created entirely by the measurement interpretation. A nonlinear torque curve should not automatically be converted using a linear-fluid formula.

Shear thinning concerns a steady stress-rate relation L9 · 16:19 L11 · 26:44

Under suitable low-shear conditions, red cells form rouleaux and larger connected aggregates. Increasing shear can break these aggregates and change cell alignment and deformation. Those microstructural changes help explain why a measured apparent viscosity falls with shear rate. The relevant apparent shear viscosity is $\eta_{\mathrm{app}}=\tau/\dot\gamma$ for the selected simple-shear protocol; the differential slope $d\tau/d\dot\gamma$ is a different quantity.

For $\tau=K\dot\gamma^n$ at positive rate, $\eta_{\mathrm{app}}=K\dot\gamma^{n-1}$ and $d\tau/d\dot\gamma=nK\dot\gamma^{n-1}$. At $0<n<1$, viscosity decreases even though stress continues to increase. Thus shear thinning does not mean that progressively faster flow must require progressively less shear stress. The power law is useful on a finite rate interval but diverges in viscosity as rate approaches zero and has no positive high-rate viscosity plateau.

The lecture quotes low/high-rate viscosity levels and compares temperatures. We retain $73.6$ and $3.5\;\mathrm{cP}$ only as source-reported illustrative levels, not as universal constants or independently reconstructed original measurements. Their ratio is $21.0286$, and $1\;\mathrm{cP}=10^{-3}\;\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Finite samples and finite observation times do not directly measure an ideal mathematical limit at exactly zero or infinite shear. The values depend on hematocrit, proteins, temperature, anticoagulant, preparation and protocol.

The conventional Newtonian plasma approximation is useful in many shear experiments. Water content alone does not prove Newtonian behavior: dissolved proteins can have flow-dependent effects. Nor does a quoted plasma viscosity of $1.2\;\mathrm{cP}$ establish a fixed percentage excess over water at every temperature. Compare solvent and sample at the same temperature and with the same measurement definition.

Elastic stress and delayed recovery require time-resolved observations L9 · 25:10 L11 · 40:04

A purely viscous instantaneous law gives zero deviatoric shear stress immediately when the local shear rate becomes zero. The material can remain in a deformed configuration without elastic restoring stress. Conversely, a viscoelastic solid may eventually recover fully after unloading but do so slowly. Partial recovery at one observation time is therefore not a definition of viscoelasticity; a permanent residual deformation may require plasticity, damage or another evolving state.

Evans and Hochmuth's 1976 membrane analysis concerns relaxation of red-cell projections created by micropipette aspiration. Its abstract identifies membrane viscosity as the dominant contribution for that experiment, rather than claiming that every red-cell deformation has the same dominant mechanism. The study helps separate a membrane property from a whole-suspension response. A pipette test requires pressure, geometry, boundary conditions and a mechanical model to turn shape history into a parameter.

For an original linear small-amplitude example, place a solvent dashpot in parallel with a Maxwell branch:

\[\tau=\eta_s\dot\gamma+\tau_p,\qquad \lambda\dot\tau_p+\tau_p=\eta_p\dot\gamma,\quad \eta_s,\eta_p,\lambda>0.\]

The dot on $\tau_p$ denotes time differentiation. In this scalar protocol $\lambda$ is a relaxation time and $\eta_p/\lambda$ is the spring modulus. The model is not asserted to be a complete objective finite-deformation blood law. Starting from steady rate $\dot\gamma_0$ and stopping at $t=0$,

\[\tau(0^-)=(\eta_s+\eta_p)\dot\gamma_0,\qquad \tau(t)=\eta_p\dot\gamma_0e^{-t/\lambda},\quad t\ge0.\]

The solvent contribution drops at once; the branch stress persists and decays. With $\eta_s=0.0035$, $\eta_p=0.0065\;\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, $\lambda=0.2\;\mathrm s$ and $\dot\gamma_0=100\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the stress changes from 1 Pa to 0.65 Pa and reaches 0.239122 Pa after 0.2 s. These numbers demonstrate what an instrument would need to resolve, not a calibration to a cited blood sample.

The normalized local estimate $-\tau/\dot\tau=\lambda$ follows for the single exponential. The magnitude of an initial slope alone cannot compare relaxation times if stress amplitudes differ. If $\tau=Ae^{-t/\lambda}$, the slope is $-A/\lambda$: doubling $A$ doubles the slope without changing $\lambda$. Baseline drift, multiple relaxation modes and instrument stopping time further complicate a fitted decay.

Frequency response supplies an independent axis of evidence L9 · 20:08 L11 · 31:56

In small-amplitude sinusoidal shear, let strain be $\gamma(t)=\gamma_a\sin\omega t$. Define storage and loss moduli by $\tau(t)=\gamma_a[G'\sin\omega t+G''\cos\omega t]$. The storage component is in phase with strain; the loss component is in phase with strain rate. Both moduli have units Pa. Fitting the scalar model above gives

\[G'=\frac{\eta_p\lambda\omega^2}{1+(\lambda\omega)^2},\qquad G''=\eta_s\omega+\frac{\eta_p\omega}{1+(\lambda\omega)^2}.\]

One way to derive them is to use complex strain $\hat\gamma e^{i\omega t}$, substitute $\dot\gamma=i\omega\gamma$ in the Maxwell equation and separate the real and imaginary parts of $i\omega\eta_p/(1+i\omega\lambda)$. At low frequency, $G'$ is quadratic in frequency while $G''$ is approximately $(\eta_s+\eta_p)\omega$. At high frequency the Maxwell storage term tends to $\eta_p/\lambda$; the solvent loss term keeps increasing.

At $\omega=5\;\mathrm{rad/s}$ with the previous parameters, $G'=0.016250\;\mathrm{Pa}$ and $G''=0.033750\;\mathrm{Pa}$. Average dissipated power per volume is $G''\omega\gamma_a^2/2\ge0$. An elastic storage signal does not require negative dissipation. Angular frequency $\omega$ and cyclic frequency $f$ differ by $\omega=2\pi f$; replacing 10 Hz by $10\;\mathrm{rad/s}$ would change the experiment.

Thurston's 1972 study measured oscillatory tube flow, including a 10-Hz series across red-cell concentrations and forcing amplitudes. Its abstract reports both viscous and elastic responses and amplitude-dependent departures from linear behavior. It therefore supports the existence and protocol dependence of whole-blood viscoelasticity, not the numerical parameters of our one-mode example or a claim that one constant relaxation time fits all samples.

A steady curve does not distinguish instantaneous thinning from structural memory

Introduce an original scalar structure fraction $a\in[0,1]$ representing a coarse aggregate state. Let

\[\dot a=k_a(1-a)-k_b|\dot\gamma|a,\qquad \eta(a)=\eta_\infty+(\eta_0-\eta_\infty)a,\qquad \tau=\eta(a)\dot\gamma.\]

Here $k_a$ has units s$^{-1}$, $k_b$ is dimensionless, and viscosity parameters have units Pa s. At $a=0$ the derivative is positive, and at $a=1$ it is nonpositive, so the interval remains invariant. For a fixed rate,

\[a_{\rm eq}=\frac{k_a}{k_a+k_b|\dot\gamma|},\qquad a(t)=a_{\rm eq}+[a(0)-a_{\rm eq}]e^{-(k_a+k_b|\dot\gamma|)t}.\]

The steady viscosity decreases with shear, but the approach to it remembers the starting structure. This time-dependent structural rheology is an example of thixotropy. Compare it with an instantaneous generalized Newtonian law $\tau=\eta(a_{\rm eq}(\dot\gamma))\dot\gamma$. The two have exactly the same entire steady curve and different transient predictions. Even perfect steady data cannot identify which model generated it.

Take $k_a=1\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $k_b=0.1$, $\eta_0=0.0736$ and $\eta_\infty=0.0035\;\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. After equilibrating at rate $1\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, a jump to $10\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ initially produces 0.672273 Pa. After equilibrating at rate $100\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the same imposed final rate initially produces 0.098727 Pa. Both tend to 0.385500 Pa with time constant 0.5 s. A preparation protocol is thus part of a reproducible viscosity measurement.

On complete cessation, this structural model has $\tau=0$ immediately because $\dot\gamma=0$, even though $a$ rebuilds towards one. It has memory but no stored shear stress. Adding a Maxwell branch would introduce a separate elastic relaxation mechanism. One should not infer elastic storage from a loop in an up/down rate sweep without examining its protocol, nor infer absence of history from a good steady-state fit.

Changing the deformation mode can expose a hidden mechanism

Brust and colleagues' 2013 plasma study provides a useful modern qualification to the course approximation. Its abstract reports Newtonian shear behavior within measurement resolution and viscoelastic behavior in capillary-breakup extension. A Newtonian shear result is therefore a bounded statement about a mode and resolution, not a complete material characterization. The study also examined a contraction/expansion microfluidic geometry; we do not reproduce that apparatus or its quantitative pressure data here.

The useful comparison is between a material time $\lambda$ and a process time $T$, expressed by $De=\lambda/T$, or between $\lambda$ and a deformation rate through $Wi=\lambda\dot\gamma$ in simple shear. These dimensionless groups describe different questions. A sample can have a high mean shear rate while also encountering a rapid contraction or a brief reversal. A high-rate viscosity plateau alone does not certify that all relevant stresses are Newtonian.

This leads naturally to experiment design and inference. Steady shear helps constrain the viscosity curve, a cessation experiment tests residual stress, rate steps with controlled preshear test structural memory, and a change of deformation mode challenges model transfer. A flexible statistical fit or neural constitutive model cannot recover a mechanism absent from the data. Its uncertainty should widen in untested modes rather than silently inherit a confident shear calibration.

from math import pi, exp, sqrt

V, A = 90.0, 135.0  # micrometre^3 and micrometre^2
rs = (3*V/(4*pi))**(1/3)
As = 4*pi*rs**2
reduced = 6*sqrt(pi)*V/A**1.5
print(f'Equal-volume sphere area: {As:.6f} um^2; reduced volume: {reduced:.6f}')
for r in (1.5, 1.3):
    length = (V-4*pi*r**3/3)/(pi*r*r)
    area = 2*V/r+4*pi*r*r/3
    assert length>0
    print(f'Spherocylinder r={r:.1f} um: length={length:.6f} um; area={area:.6f} um^2; area budget permits={area<=A}')

P50, n, capacity = 26.0, 2.7, 20.0
S = lambda p: p**n/(P50**n+p**n)
released=capacity*(S(100)-S(40))
print(f'Toy saturation: S(100)={S(100):.6f}; S(40)={S(40):.6f}; released={released:.6f} mL/dL')

mu, omega, R, h = .0012, 10.0, .02, .001
T=pi*mu*omega*R**4/(2*h)
assert abs(2*h*T/(pi*omega*R**4)-mu)<1e-15
nflow,K=.5,.02
Tpower=2*pi*K*(omega/h)**nflow*R**(nflow+3)/(nflow+3)
rim=K*(omega*R/h)**nflow
corrected=(3+nflow)*Tpower/(2*pi*R**3)
assert abs(corrected-rim)<1e-14
print(f'Newtonian torque: {T:.9e} N m; power-law naive/correct rim stress: {4/(3+nflow):.6f}')

eta_s,eta_p,lam,gamma=.0035,.0065,.2,100.0
initial=(eta_s+eta_p)*gamma
justafter=eta_p*gamma
atlam=justafter*exp(-1)
assert abs(-atlam/(-atlam/lam)-lam)<1e-15
omega=5.
Gp=eta_p*lam*omega**2/(1+(lam*omega)**2)
Gpp=eta_s*omega+eta_p*omega/(1+(lam*omega)**2)
print(f'Maxwell stop: before={initial:.6f} Pa; after={justafter:.6f} Pa; at lambda={atlam:.6f} Pa')

ka,kb,eta0,etainf=1.,.1,.0736,.0035

assert abs(astep(20)-aeq(10))<1e-14

Problems and fully worked solutions

1. Audit a density and viscosity statement. Convert $1.05\;\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ and $3.5\;\mathrm{cP}$ to SI. What is wrong with calling $1050\;\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ the same density?

Solution

A cubic centimetre is $10^{-6}\;\mathrm{m^3}$ and one gram is $10^{-3}\;\mathrm{kg}$, so the density is $1050\;\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. The viscosity is $0.0035\;\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. In contrast, $1050\;\mathrm{g/cm^3}=1.05\times10^6\;\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, a thousand times the intended value. The correct numerical value must be paired with the correct unit; verbal plausibility cannot repair a conversion error.

2. Separate count and volume. A model sample contains $4.8\times10^6$ red cells per microlitre, each averaging $95\;\mathrm{fL}$. Compute hematocrit. If cell count is unchanged and mean volume decreases by 10%, how does it change?

Solution

The red-cell volume per microlitre is $4.8\times10^6(95)=4.56\times10^8\;\mathrm{fL}$, giving $H=0.456$. Reducing mean volume to $85.5\;\mathrm{fL}$ gives $H=0.4104$. The count has not changed, but the volume fraction has. This is an algebraic suspension example; it does not specify why the volume changed or interpret a clinical test.

3. Identify the Hill slope. At saturation $S=1/2$ in the toy oxygen model, find $dS/d\log P$. Would halving the capacity $C_*$ change the saturation curve or the oxygen-content difference?

Solution

The derivative is $nS(1-S)=n/4=0.675$ for $n=2.7$. Capacity does not enter $S(P)$, so this stipulated curve is unchanged. However, $C_*(S_a-S_v)$ is halved, from $4.248975$ to $2.1244875\;\mathrm{mL/dL}$ for the stated endpoints. Saturation and oxygen content must not be conflated.

4. Test a shape constraint. Derive the spherocylinder area formula by eliminating $\ell$. What can be concluded when it exceeds the available area?

Solution

From volume, $\ell=V_c/(\pi r^2)-4r/3$. Substitution into $2\pi r\ell+4\pi r^2$ yields $2V_c/r+4\pi r^2/3$. If the required area exceeds the budget, the selected spherocylinder cannot have simultaneously that radius, volume and unchanged area. Area dilation, volume change or a different shape would be needed. The comparison is not a theorem excluding every confined shape, and does not determine the required pressure or cell survival.

5. Correct a non-Newtonian torque. In a steady power-law experiment, $T$ scales as $\Omega^{1/2}$. What rim-stress correction replaces the Newtonian formula, and by what percentage does the latter err?

Solution

The logarithmic slope is $n=1/2$, so $\Omega T'=T/2$. Hence $\tau_R=3.5T/(2\pi R^3)$. The Newtonian value is $4T/(2\pi R^3)$, a ratio $4/3.5=8/7$. It overestimates the correct rim stress by $1/7=14.2857\%$. This result assumes a single steady shear law and the ideal geometry; a slope influenced by structural drift would invalidate that inference.

6. Does thinning prove elasticity? For $\tau=K\dot\gamma^{1/2}$ at positive rates, compare apparent and differential viscosities and determine stress immediately after cessation in the instantaneous model.

Solution

The apparent viscosity is $K\dot\gamma^{-1/2}$ and the differential viscosity is half that value. Both decrease with rate while $\tau$ increases. Extending the stress law continuously to zero gives zero stress on cessation. It therefore shear-thins without storing an elastic shear stress. The apparent viscosity diverges as rate tends to zero, but the stress itself tends to zero; this is one reason the unregularized law should be restricted to a finite fitting interval.

7. Estimate relaxation without confusing amplitude. Two cessation traces are $Ae^{-t/0.2}$ and $2Ae^{-t/0.2}$. Which relaxes faster? For the worked Maxwell example, compute the time for the residual branch stress to fall to 5% of its immediate post-stop value.

Solution

The two normalized traces are identical, with relaxation time 0.2 s. The second initial slope is twice as large only because its amplitude is twice as large. The 5% time solves $e^{-t/0.2}=0.05$, so $t=0.2\log20=0.599146\;\mathrm s$. This fraction refers to the residual 0.65-Pa branch stress, not the 1-Pa pre-stop total. The solvent jump and branch relaxation are distinct.

8. Predict structural recovery at rest. For the aggregation toy model after preshear $100\;\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, find $a(t)$ when shear is stopped. Is there a residual shear stress? How long until $a=0.9$?

Solution

Initially $a=1/11$. At rest, $\dot a=1-a$, giving $a(t)=1-(10/11)e^{-t}$ with time in seconds. The stress is zero throughout because $\tau=\eta(a)\dot\gamma$ and the rate is zero. Solving $0.1=(10/11)e^{-t}$ gives $t=\log(100/11)=2.207275\;\mathrm s$. Structure continues to evolve without an elastic shear-stress tail; its evolution will affect a later restart.

9. Design a discrimination experiment. An instantaneous viscosity model and a structural model fit every available steady rate exactly. Specify measurements that could distinguish them and explain why an additional steady point is insufficient.

Solution

Use the same sample and final rate after two controlled preshear histories, record the immediate and long-time torque, and account for the rheometer's acceleration response. The structural example predicts different initial stresses converging to the same steady value; the instantaneous model predicts the same stress once the final rate is reached. Add a cessation measurement to test for a residual elastic stress, which the structural model alone lacks. An extra steady point cannot distinguish models deliberately constructed to share their entire steady curve. Changing to extension further tests transfer to an uncalibrated deformation mode, but requires its own geometry and uncertainty analysis.
