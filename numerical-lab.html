Numerical laboratory: a vessel supplies a tissue bed

A radius measurement becomes a prediction only after we specify what drives the flow and what the tissue consumes. Change those assumptions and the same narrowing gives a different mechanical answer.

This laboratory implements the original steady tube–oxygen model developed in chapter 1. Its inputs are illustrative. It does not model a particular patient's anatomy, collateral circulation, autoregulation or treatment response.

Model, units and reference state

A rigid circular tube has radius $a$, length $L=0.10\,\mathrm m$ and Newtonian viscosity $\mu=0.004\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Its reference radius is $a_0=1\,\mathrm{mm}$ and its reference pressure drop is $\Delta p_0=1000\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Fully developed, steady, laminar flow gives

\[Q=\frac{\pi a^4\Delta p}{8\mu L},\qquad \tau_w=\frac{\Delta p a}{2L}=\frac{4\mu Q}{\pi a^3}.\]

The reference whole-blood oxygen contents are $C_a=9$ and $C_{v,0}=6\,\mathrm{mmol/L}$. A well-mixed tissue consumes oxygen at $M_0=Q_0(C_a-C_{v,0})$. At steady state and equal inlet/outlet volume flow, conservation requires $C_v=C_a-M/Q$. A negative calculated $C_v$ signals failure of the prescribed steady supply–demand state. It is not a physical negative concentration.

Read the result as a conditional prediction

Under fixed pressure, $Q/Q_0=\alpha^4$ and $\tau_w/\tau_{w,0}=\alpha$, where $\alpha=a/a_0$. At half the radius, the model supplies one sixteenth of the original flow and half the original wall shear stress. Under fixed flow, $\Delta p/\Delta p_0=\alpha^{-4}$ and $\tau_w/\tau_{w,0}=\alpha^{-3}$: the required pressure rises sixteenfold and the wall shear rises eightfold. These are two different boundary-value problems.

At fixed demand, the largest possible oxygen extraction is one. Hence a necessary condition for a nonnegative venous oxygen content is $Q C_a\ge M$. The indicator reports only that algebraic capacity check. Real tissue function also depends on diffusion distance, transit time, heterogeneous capillary recruitment, binding kinetics and cellular energetics; a positive outlet content does not establish adequate oxygenation.

Reproduce the reference result in Python

The independently executable listing below uses SI units for mechanics and converts to litres per minute before the oxygen balance. Changing units in only one part of the calculation creates a thousandfold or millionfold error.

from math import pi
radius, length, viscosity, dp = 0.001, 0.10, 0.004, 1000.0
q_si = pi * radius**4 * dp / (8 * viscosity * length)
q = q_si * 60000  # m^3/s to L/min
ca, cv = 9.0, 6.0  # mmol/L of whole blood
consumption = q * (ca - cv)
wall_shear = dp * radius / (2 * length)
print(f"Flow: {q:.6f} L/min")
print(f"Wall shear: {wall_shear:.3f} Pa")
print(f"Consumption: {consumption:.6f} mmol/min")
print(f"Minimum fixed-pressure radius ratio: {((ca-cv)/ca)**0.25:.6f}")

Flow: 0.058905 L/min
Wall shear: 5.000 Pa
Consumption: 0.176715 mmol/min
Minimum fixed-pressure radius ratio: 0.759836

The last number follows from $\alpha^4\ge(C_a-C_{v,0})/C_a=1/3$. It belongs to this artificial tube, fixed demand and fixed pressure. It is not a clinical stenosis threshold.

A question to test the model

Can a parallel bypass restore the original flow when the unchanged downstream resistance still dominates the circuit?

Worked answer

Write upstream resistance $R_s$, bypass resistance $R_g$ and downstream resistance $R_m$. The bypass changes the upstream part to $R_{\parallel}=R_sR_g/(R_s+R_g)$, giving $Q=\Delta p/(R_{\parallel}+R_m)$. Even as $R_g\to0$, the limiting flow is $\Delta p/R_m$. A bypass cannot remove the downstream term. For $R_s=4$, $R_m=1$, $R_g=4$ in common resistance units, the total resistance falls from 5 to 3, and flow increases by $5/3$. If $R_m$ were 100 instead, the factor would be $104/102$, only about 1.020. The useful question is which element limits the complete circuit.
